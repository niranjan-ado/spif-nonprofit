Overcoming Childhood Trauma: The Complete Guide

print Print Article

What is childhood trauma?

Childhood trauma can be defined as an adverse childhood experience that involves potential abuse of any form, neglect or adversity that negatively impacts a child.[1] Having an adverse childhood experience increases the likelihood of consuming harmful substances, and experiencing chronic conditions like pulmonary or cardiac disease later in life.[2] Because childhood trauma can have a profound impact on an individual across almost all areas of development, intentionally addressing it is crucial to active healing.[3]

Why is it important to overcome childhood trauma?

warning Understanding the Impact
  • radio_button_checked Experiencing a traumatic event in childhood can disrupt a person's current ability to build and sustain healthy interpersonal relationships and secure attachments.[4]
  • radio_button_checked Traumatic events consistently trigger deep survival responses; remaining locked in these chronic threat loops severely drains day-to-day somatic functioning.[5]
  • radio_button_checked Survivors may constantly feel threatened and uncomfortable even when sitting in objectively safe, calm environments, which drastically lowers their long-term focus and productivity.[6]
  • radio_button_checked Essential physiological functions like sleep and digestion are frequently compromised by regular survival triggers, which often manifests as clinical insomnia or eating disorders.[7]
psychology

How to overcome childhood trauma

Healing is not about erasing your history; it is about changing how that history affects your present body and mind. Here are ten evidence-based strategies for recovery:

  • visibility 1. Recognizing the trauma The first action towards any issue is to recognize the problem. Very often, people with adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) do not recognize the symptoms of trauma they are showing. Research indicates that people who have experienced trauma are unlikely to spontaneously disclose their traumatic experience, making it difficult for the mental health practitioner to understand the symptoms. Recognizing trauma and the symptoms one is showing is therefore important.[8][9]
  • done_all 2. Acceptance Accepting the reality that certain individuals have hurt you and that has led to intentional or unintentional trauma is a vital step toward reclaiming your life.[10]
  • favorite 3. Being kind to oneself Guilt and criticising oneself is very common in those who have experienced trauma. Blaming oneself for the events that happened is also seen. Being kind and less self-critical is crucial to repairing self-worth.[11]
  • fitness_center 4. Looking after one's health Having a healthy body helps in coping with stress. Building a stable daily routine, eating well, and practising meditation or regular exercise helps buffer against metabolic stress responses.[12]
  • block 5. Dismantling harmful habits Having experienced a traumatic event might cause an individual to rely on substances, process emotions through junk food, or severely compromise on sleep. Substituting these avoidance tactics with health-promoting behaviors like attending group sessions or cultivating reading habits helps build resilience.[13]
  • forum 6. Sharing your experiences Sharing is a very important and powerful tool when used properly. Disclosing your struggles to trusted individuals in a safe environment is a core pillar of processing traumatic stress.[14]
  • person 7. Reclaiming authority You are not your trauma. Trauma is just a part of your historical experience—it does not dictate your current value or define your future capabilities.[15]
  • support_agent 8. Reaching out for help Memories of trauma can be a very isolating experience. Proactively reach out to safe friends and family members, and avoid the urge to pull away into isolation.[16]
  • trending_up 9. Focusing on post-traumatic growth Healing is an unlinear process; although it may disrupt some of your days, you will learn to navigate it with healthier coping tools. Focus intentionally on the secure relationships you have built, your personal achievements, and your inner strengths.[17]
  • medical_services 10. Seeking out professional help Sometimes you need the guidance of a professional to safely process trauma. Collate reliable trauma-informed resources, reach out to a certified specialist (practicing EMDR or somatics), or join a moderated support group.[18]

Need immediate support?

Processing childhood trauma can bring up intense, overwhelming emotions. If you are experiencing a crisis, feeling unsafe, or having thoughts of self-harm, please reach out for professional support immediately. You don't have to face this alone.
